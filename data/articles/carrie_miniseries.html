Carrie is an American supernatural horror television series developed by Mike Flanagan, and produced by Amazon MGM Studios for Amazon Prime Video. The fourth screen adaptation in the Carrie franchise, it is the first serial adaptation of the 1974 novel by Stephen King, and expands the storyline. The series follows Carrie White (Summer H. Howell), a teenage girl who has spent her life in seclusion, as she is forced to adjust to high school following her father's death. She learns to control her mysterious telekinetic powers during the events leading up to her infamous rampage. It also stars Samantha Sloyan, Siena Agudong, Alison Thornton, Joel Oulette, Josie Totah, Arthur Conti, Thalia Dudek, Amber Midthunder, and Matthew Lillard.

Plans for a television series adaptation of the novel started in 2002 with a television film intended as a backdoor pilot by MGM Television but later passed on by NBC. In 2019, a miniseries was in development at FX but was never ordered by the network. Amazon announced a new project's development for Amazon Prime Video in 2024, with Flanagan serving as showrunner, while King joined as an executive producer. It was given a series order in April 2025. Principal photography occurred in Vancouver, Canada, from June to October 2025.

Carrie premiered on October 7, 2026, on Amazon Prime Video. The series received positive reviews from critics, who praised Flanagan's modern take on the source material, themes, and the performances of the cast (particularly Howell and Sloyan), but some were divided over its changes from the novel. Originally billed as a miniseries, the series was renewed for a second season in October 2026.

Premise

[edit]

Carrie is described as a reimagining of the story of misfit high schooler Carrie White (Summer H. Howell), who has spent her life in seclusion with her domineering mother (Samantha Sloyan). After her father's sudden death, Carrie finds herself contending with the challenges of attending high school, a bullying scandal, and the emergence of mysterious telekinetic powers.[1]

— Deadline Hollywood

Production

[edit]

Development

[edit]

In December 2022, Mike Flanagan signed a first-look overall TV deal with Amazon Studios.[2] Upon striking the deal, Flanagan immediately tried to launch a televised adaptation of Stephen King's novel series The Dark Tower, a passion project of his he had discussed repeatedly over the years, but Flanagan found the project "one of the most complicated properties to get started" because receiving approval was a much longer and involved process than anything he had ever approached in his life, but then Amazon brought to him the idea to launch their partnership with an adaptation of King's novel Carrie (1974), with Flanagan proposing a new take that excited even King, leading him to assemble his frequent collaborators to do so.[3] In October 2024, it was announced that Flanagan's first project under that deal would be a new adaptation of King's Carrie novel, in the form of an eight episode miniseries, at Amazon Prime Video. A writers' room was launched to flesh out the story. Flanagan had previously helmed adaptations of King's novels including Gerald's Game (2017), Doctor Sleep (2019), and The Life of Chuck (2024).[4] Around this time, Flanagan pitched the DC Universe (DCU) film Clayface (2026) to James Gunn and Peter Safran for DC Studios, which he developed and wrote, but when it came time to start production on it, Flanagan was unavailable to direct it due to his commitments to Carrie and The Exorcist: Martyrs (2027), resulting in James Watkins being hired instead.[5] Flanagan served as writer and showrunner on the series, in addition to directing four episodes. King also executive produces the series.[6] In October 2026, Amazon Prime Video renewed the series for a second season.[7]

Writing

[edit]

Due to the original Carrie novel being one of King's shortest books, Flanagan expanded the story to suit a long-form series format. Flanagan acknowledged previous Carrie adaptations, including Brian De Palma's 1976 film and Kimberly Peirce's 2013 film, but instead of revisiting familiar narrative ground, he sought to build something new out of the story's ingredients.[3] He envisioned Carrie as being a three-season show.[8]

Flanagan chose to emphasize the novel's themes of bullying, school violence, and kindness versus cruelty due to society's changing relationship with technology and the increased degree of school violence in the United States in the decades since the novel was published.[3] Teenagers, including Flanagan's son Rigby, were brought into the writers' room in order for Flanagan and his team to better understand contemporary adolescence and high school experiences.[9] Flanagan further built out from the book's mythology and featured the chapters including panicked 911 calls, police reports, courtroom testimonies, and scientific investigations that attempted to explain Carrie's phenomenon, which implied Carrie was part of a sorority of gifted women who had telekinetic powers.[3] Flanagan opted to run with that plot point starting from the first episode, depicting a different, unique story of a different woman somewhere in the world coming to terms with her abilities while Carrie finds her specific place among them over the season.[3]

Unlike previous versions, Flanagan's version of Carrie White has spent her life hidden away at home with her mother Margaret, with the state unaware of their existence until the sudden death of Carrie's father and Margaret's husband Ralph, forcing a spotlight onto their household that prompts Carrie to enter public school for the first time in her life. In addition, he added new aspects to the story's principal characters.[3] Whereas De Palma's Carrie was a "clenched fist who's absolutely shaking out there in the world", Flanagan's Carrie is "wide open", "eager", "curious" and "earnest", walking into her teenage years without any sense of danger but with the innate trust in people and goodness.[3] Instead of portraying Margaret White as the tyrannical parental abuser of King's novel and De Palma's film, Flanagan chose to emphasize Margaret's love for her daughter and need to protect her from the world's dangers. Despite the changes to the original novel, Flanagan confirmed that core events and characters will be maintained, such as Chris Hargensen, Sue Snell, Tommy Ross, Billy Nolan, and Rita Desjardin, as well as the period scene at the bathroom and the Black Prom.[3] A new character introduced in Flanagan's version is Emaline.[3]

Casting

[edit]

Over 1,400 actresses auditioned for the lead role of Carrie White in what Flanagan described as one of the biggest casting searches of his career.[10] During auditions, many of the candidates attempted to emulate Sissy Spacek's performance from the 1976 film, despite the production team's desire to avoid similarities. Summer H. Howell submitted a self-tape audition in January 2025, rising to the top due to not being informed by other adaptations of Carrie.[9] Flanagan commented that Howell "playing that earnestness, kindness and eagerness without feeling naive" was what impressed him in her audition tape.[9] Her performance impressed the team so much that she was cast, despite her physical similarities to Spacek at the time.[3] In April 2025, Howell was reported to be in talks to join as Carrie,[11] and that Siena Agudong was cast as Sue Snell.[12] Howell first spoke with Flanagan on April Fools' Day, believing that it was a prank with a fake job offer.[10] In May, Matthew Lillard was in talks to join the series in an undisclosed role and Howell was officially cast as Carrie.[13]

In June, Lillard was confirmed to join the series, alongside Samantha Sloyan, Alison Thornton, Thalia Dudek, Amber Midthunder, Josie Totah, Arthur Conti, Joel Oulette, Kate Siegel, Michael Trucco, Katee Sackhoff, Rahul Kohli, Tim Bagley, Heather Graham, Tahmoh Penikett, Crystal Balint, Danielle Klaudt, Mapuana Makia, Rowan Danielle, Naika Toussaint, Delainey Hayles and Cassandra Naud.[1][14] Flanagan wrote Margaret's role specifically for Sloyan, deeming it "the part she was born to play."[3] Thalia Dudek also joined the cast as new character Emaline.[3]

Filming

[edit]

Principal photography began in Vancouver on June 16, 2025.[15][16] Eric Hamber Secondary School in Vancouver stood in for Ewen High School.[17] Cloverdale represented the town of Chamberlain with scenes of the town on fire being filmed there in mid-July 2025.[18] Exterior shots of the Whites' house was shot at 821 Henley Street in New Westminster while Sue Snell's house was shot at 206 Queens Avenue in New Westminster.[19] Scenes with Carrie walking home while mailboxes opened behind her were shot on 15th Avenue in Burnaby.[19] Bar scenes featuring Ewen's teachers were filmed at El Camino's on 3250 Main Street in Vancouver.[19] Midway through production, the prom scene in the series' final two episodes was filmed over a two-week block with Flanagan calling the scene "the most difficult sequence of my career" to film.[20] Howell estimated that she spent 12 days covered in blood while filming the scene.[20] Filming concluded on October 25, 2025.[21]

Music

[edit]

On July 13, 2026, the Newton Brothers was announced to compose the series.[22] The series soundtrack album was released on October 7, 2026, by Milan Records.[23] Singer Ruelle performed the song "Slow Motion Memory Fade" was released as a single on October 1, 2026.[24] It was featured in the first and last episodes of the series,[25] with an orchestral versions of the song by the Newton Brothers appeared in the soundtrack.[26] 

The alt-pop duo Haute and Freddy became the initial musical identity and backbone of the show and Flanagan even rewrote episode 8 around their song "Shy Girl".[27][28] Haute and Freddy's nearly entire 2026 album Big Disgrace is used all over the series. Carrie wears their t-shirt in episodes two and three;[28][29][27] she admits she doesn't know what it is, and Miss Desjardin replies that they're "a very cool band".

Release

[edit]

Carrie was released globally with all eight episodes on Amazon Prime Video on October 7, 2026.[30][31] Before the global release, the series premiered in the "Primetime" program at the 2026 Toronto International Film Festival on September 18.[32]

Critical response

[edit]

The review aggregator website Rotten Tomatoes reported an 85% approval rating, based on 133 reviews, with an average of rated reviews of 7.2/10. The website's critics consensus reads, "Mike Flanagan aptly expands this teen mythos with added compassion and ingenuity, bringing a modern touch to a timeless work and giving all performers involved the opportunity to shine while witnessing the ascension of a fascinatingly new Carrie."[33] Metacritic, which uses a weighted average, assigned a score of 72 out of 100 based on 33 critics, indicating "generally favorable" reviews.[34]

Jordan Williams from Screen Rant praised Flanagan for justifying the miniseries' existence as yet another adaptation of King's first novel despite the existence of de Palma's film due to compellingly transforming King's themes and characters into an even more realized, nuanced portrait of the complex intersection of fear, anger and compassion in society through modernization and expanding on what the book originally showed.[35] Likewise, Judy Berman in Time magazine called Carrie the first worthwhile adaptation of the novel in 50 years as Flanagan makes it relevant to current times with the series representing "a product of the Trump decade".[36]

Nate Richard of Collider noted how many adaptations of King's works are notoriously "hits or misses" on the hands of different auteurs, but Flanagan is an exception and that, in regards to whether the property should be "dead or better", his miniseries proved to be a new take that, while imperfect, was highly addictive and feels more like a remix than a retelling.[37]

Others were more critical of the new additions to the source material. In The Hollywood Reporter, Daniel Fienberg wrote, "Flanagan is extending and over-extending the text in ways that are fairly conventional, but with a story as familiar and previously simple as Carrie, those changes could be interpreted as surprising, if not necessarily creative."[38] He continued, "In the process, some of the engine that steers the property toward its explosive conclusion is lost. Even if you don’t know the story in any version, you know something bad is coming at the prom, but when it comes around, it isn't as disturbing or terrifying as it ought to be."[38]

Emma Stefansky of The Atlantic said "the show is almost too empathetic to its main characters; it doesn't allow them to revel in their spitefulness, as they do in the source material. It treats many of them as victims of larger forces beyond their control—and robs viewers of the catharsis of Carrie's big moment".[39] She concluded, "Defanging Carrie means flinching away from the unforgiving bleakness that makes the story so memorable."[39] In The Times, James Jackson lauded the series' timeliness but commented "an overprotracted finale and various other superfluous additions to the story did made me long for the lean, mean concision of the original film".[40]

Audience response

[edit]

Prior to the premiere, some fans expressed disappointment with the show's physical portrayal of the protagonist, citing that no screen adaptation had matched the novel's description, in which Carrie is described as "a chunky girl with pimples".[41] People note that adaptations of Carrie have cast actresses that are conventionally attractive, which some feel goes against King's original interpretation of Carrie.[41][42][43] A number of critics and fans expressed their desire to see an adaptation that fully explored Carrie as originally written by King.[41][43]
